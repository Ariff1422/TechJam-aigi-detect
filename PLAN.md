## 1. Model & architecture decision

### 1.1 Backbone: frozen CLIP ViT, not a CNN trained from scratch

**Decision:** use a pretrained CLIP vision encoder (`open_clip`, ViT-L/14 or ViT-B/32), completely frozen, with only a small trainable head on top.

**Why, with numbers:**

- Ojha et al. (CVPR 2023, "Towards Universal Fake Image Detectors that Generalize Across Generative Models") showed that training a classifier from scratch causes it to overfit to fake-specific artifacts, treating "real" as a catch-all sink class that fails on unseen generator families. Their fix — a linear probe on frozen CLIP features — reaches cross-generator average precision > 0.9.
- A controlled comparison (Mixture of Low-Rank Experts, arXiv 2404.04883) trained multiple methods on identical ProGAN data and tested across GAN and diffusion generators:

| Method                           | mAP on GANs                | mAP on Diffusion           | Consistency                         |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| CNNSpot (plain CNN + aug)        | 94.19%                     | 76.22%                     | Large gap — blind spot on diffusion |
| DIRE (reconstruction error)      | 85.84%                     | 95.95%                     | Large gap — blind spot on GANs      |
| **UnivFD (CLIP + linear probe)** | **93.38% (both settings)** | **93.38% (both settings)** | **No blind spot**                   |

This is the single most important number in this document: the CLIP approach is the _only_ one without a generator family it fails badly on. Since you don't know what's in the private evaluation set, that consistency matters more than squeezing out a few extra points on one family.

This consistency shows up specifically under the kind of distortions this track tests, too: a comparison of detectors under Gaussian blur (RA-Det, arXiv 2603.01544) found pixel-artifact-based detectors collapsing sharply — one dropped from 55.1% to 51.0% accuracy, another from 57.8% to 53.5% — while the CLIP-feature-based detector (UniFD) declined far more gently, from 71.3% to 67.9%. This is direct evidence that the architecture choice itself, independent of your augmentation strategy, matters for the robustness this track is scoring.

### 1.1b Counter-evidence: DINOv2 is a legitimate alternative — don't skip this

The picture above isn't one-sided, and pretending otherwise would be overconfident. Two papers (arXiv 2605.04358, "Intermediate Representations are Strong AI-Generated Image Detectors"; arXiv 2603.21619) found CLIP clearly outperforming DINOv2 on the controlled GenImage benchmark — in one case by a large AUROC/AP margin across multiple generators. But a more recent paper (PROBE, arXiv 2605.24906) tested generalization specifically to **in-the-wild, social-media-style images** (Chameleon, WildRF, SynthWildX benchmarks) — the exact kind of "posted online, degraded, unpredictable source" framing this track uses — and found a DINOv2-based method beating CLIP-based UnivFD by a wide margin (e.g. 90.3% vs. 57.5% average accuracy on WildRF).

**Takeaway:** CLIP's advantage shows up on clean lab benchmarks; DINOv2's advantage shows up on messy, realistic conditions closer to what this track actually simulates. Since neither backbone requires retraining anything beyond the small head (Section 3.3's caching trick applies to both identically), don't treat this as a single up-front decision — treat it as a cheap experiment:

- **Phase 4 (train the real model):** train and validate with CLIP first, per the rest of this section — it remains the lower-risk default to get a working result.
- **Phase 5/7 — attempted, closed out (2026-08-27):** re-ran feature extraction with a frozen DINOv2 (`dinov2_vitl14`) and confirmed it loads correctly (304M params, 1024-dim embeddings, both matching this section's estimates). Full-scale caching to match CLIP's comparison was not completed: measured throughput was ~63x slower than CLIP per image (2.835 sec/image vs. 0.045 sec/version), driven by DINOv2's 518x518 input resolution (~1370 tokens/image) versus CLIP's 224x224 (~50 tokens) — a property of the model family, not something a smaller DINOv2 variant would fix, since token count doesn't shrink with parameter count. Even a scoped-down clean-only comparison (2,800 images, no augmentation multiplier) still projected to ~2.2 hours, over the pre-agreed stop threshold on CPU-only hardware — dropped for this hackathon rather than run past budget. GPU access (an open question since Phase 3, never revisited) would resolve this directly; see the Changelog entry for the full numbers. This is a genuine hardware/compute limitation, not a finding against DINOv2 itself — the comparison remains a legitimate open question for anyone with GPU access.

**Stronger, more recent evidence (elevates this from optional to a priority experiment):** the NTIRE 2026 Challenge on Robust AI-Generated Image Detection in the Wild (CVPR 2026 workshop) — a near-identical task, run at competition scale with 511 participants — had top-placing teams using a DINOv3 backbone fine-tuned with LoRA rather than a frozen CLIP linear probe. Independently, the MoLE paper (arXiv 2404.04883) found LoRA-adapted CLIP beating plain linear-probe UnivFD by up to +12.72% accuracy. Two more-recent, independent results now point the same direction: a lightly fine-tuned adapter (LoRA) on a strong backbone beats a frozen linear head. See Section 7 — this moved from "stretch goal if time allows" to "worth trying early."

### 1.2 Head architecture: linear probe first, small MLP or adapter as upgrade

- **MVP:** single linear layer, input dimension equal to the backbone's output embedding size, output 1, sigmoid. Verify the actual dimension with a shape check rather than assuming — for the OpenAI-pretrained weights, CLIP ViT-B/32 outputs **512-dim** embeddings and CLIP ViT-L/14 outputs **768-dim** embeddings (not the reverse). This is exactly Ojha et al.'s setup and is proven, fast to train, and trivial to implement correctly — but only if the input dimension is right, so print `embedding.shape` once before wiring up the head.
- **Stretch upgrade (only after MVP works end-to-end):** "CLIPping the Deception" (arXiv 2402.12927) shows that prompt-tuning or a small adapter network on the same frozen CLIP features beats the plain linear probe by **+5.01% mAP and +6.61% accuracy** across 18 generators, while training far fewer parameters than full fine-tuning. If you have time left after the core pipeline is solid, swap the linear head for a 2-layer MLP (`embedding_dim → 256 → 1`, ReLU, dropout 0.3) — this is a low-risk, well-evidenced upgrade path, not a gamble. Keep `embedding_dim` as a config value, not a hardcoded number, since it changes if you swap backbones (Section 1.1b): CLIP ViT-B/32 = 512, CLIP ViT-L/14 = 768, DINOv2 ViT-L/14 ≈ 1024 — always verify with a shape check rather than trusting any number written down, including these.
- **Do not fine-tune the CLIP backbone itself.** No published result in this survey shows it's necessary, it multiplies your training time, and it risks overfitting to your (likely small) training subset.

### 1.3 Model size vs. the 2B parameter cap

CLIP ViT-B/32 (~151M params) and ViT-L/14 (~428M params) are both comfortably under the cap. DINOv2 ViT-L/14 (~300M params) is also comfortably under. Since the backbone is frozen (or, for the LoRA stretch goal in Section 7, only lightly adapted), its size only affects one-time feature extraction cost per image, not per-epoch training cost — so there's little reason to pick the smaller one except for faster iteration early on.

**⚠️ Parameter-cap trap to avoid:** DINOv3 is released in multiple sizes, from roughly 86M parameters up to a **7-billion-parameter** variant. At least one paper referenced in this document (Section 1.1b) used that 7B version. If you pick DINOv3 (per the NTIRE 2026 finding in Section 1.1b), explicitly use a small/base/large variant (e.g. `dinov3_vitl16`, comparable in size to CLIP ViT-L/14) — never the 7B one, which alone would blow through the entire 2B cap. Confirm and state your final model's total parameter count explicitly in the README regardless of which backbone you land on.

**Recommendation:** ViT-B/32 while building/debugging the pipeline (Phase 1–2 below), switch to ViT-L/14 for the final submission (Phase 5) — same code, one config value changed.

### 1.4 A specific failure mode to design around

"Improved Detection of Latent Diffusion-Generated Images through Aligned Datasets" (arXiv 2410.11835) found a CLIP-linear-probe variant had spuriously learned that _downsampled images are more likely real_ — because in their training data, real images happened to be more downsampled than fakes on average.

**This is not about training-time resize augmentation** — Resize is a held-out transform (Section 3.2) that's deliberately never applied during training, so it can't be the source of this shortcut in this plan. The actual risk is upstream: if your raw SID_Set subsample happens to have a systematic native-resolution difference between the real (OpenImages V7) and synthetic (FLUX-generated) images before any augmentation touches them, the model can learn "smaller/blurrier native resolution → real" as a shortcut that has nothing to do with actual generation artifacts — and this shortcut would then be invisible until you test the held-out Resize transform in Phase 5, at which point it would look like a robustness failure when it's really a data-bias failure. **Mitigation:** during Phase 3, plot or tabulate the native resolution distribution of your real vs. fake subsample before training. If there's a systematic gap, resize every image (both classes, identically) to a common fixed resolution as a preprocessing step before feature extraction, so native resolution can never become a usable signal for either class.

### 2.2 Splitting strategy

1. Subsample SID_Set to a manageable working size — 15,000–20,000 images total is enough to get a real signal without multi-hour training runs. This isn't an arbitrary shortcut: a lightweight CLIP-based detector has been shown to generalize well from as little as a handful of training examples per generator, matching or beating prior state of the art on out-of-distribution data (+6% AUC) and on degraded/laundered images (+13%) (Cozzolino et al., "Raising the Bar of AI-generated Image Detection with CLIP," arXiv 2312.00195) — so don't feel pressure to scale this up; put the saved time into the Phase 4b/5 stretch experiments instead.
2. Drop the "tampered" class entirely (Section 0).
3. Split **70% train / 15% val / 15% test**, stratified so every split is roughly 50:50 real:fake.
4. **Split to prevent near-duplicate leakage — but know what SID_Set actually is before assuming "split by generator" helps.** SID_Set's synthetic images are generated by a single model, FLUX (per Huang et al., the paper introducing SID_Set) — so unlike a multi-generator dataset (WildFake, GenImage), there usually isn't a "generator" axis to split on here, and advice to do so wouldn't meaningfully reduce leakage for this specific dataset. The real leakage risk is different: (a) near-duplicate or highly similar images within the OpenImages-V7-sourced real set, and (b) possible content correlation between a real image and a synthetic image if the synthetic set's generation prompts were derived from real image captions. Concretely: **before splitting, run a perceptual-hash (pHash) or embedding-similarity pass across your full subsample** and group any near-duplicate pairs into the same split rather than letting them land on opposite sides of the train/test boundary. If SID_Set's actual metadata (once downloaded) does include explicit source/generation-batch identifiers, use those instead — but don't assume it will; the pHash/embedding fallback works regardless.
5. Keep the split assignment in a checked-in CSV/JSON (`splits/train.txt`, `splits/val.txt`, `splits/test.txt`) so it's reproducible and referee-able from your README.
6. Set and log a fixed random seed for the subsampling and split (e.g. `seed=42`, stored in `configs/default.yaml`) — the README's required "steps to reproduce" section depends on this being deterministic, not re-randomized on every run.

### 2.3 What NOT to do

- Don't touch the hidden/private evaluation data — there isn't one here (unlike Track 2), but don't accidentally evaluate on images you trained on.
- Don't over-sample one class heavily without accounting for it in your metrics — report AUC (threshold-independent) alongside accuracy so class balance choices don't quietly inflate your numbers.

## 3. Augmentation & robustness strategy

### 3.1 The exact transform grid (from the brief — implement this once, reuse everywhere)

| Transform        | Parameters                            |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| JPEG Compression | quality = 90, 70, 50, 30              |
| Gaussian Blur    | σ = 0.5, 1.0, 2.0                     |
| Resize           | scale 0.5× / 0.25×, then upscale back |
| Gaussian Noise   | σ = 0.02, 0.05, 0.10                  |
| Color Jitter     | brightness/contrast/saturation ±20%   |
| Center Crop      | crop to 80%                           |

### 3.2 Train/held-out split of the transform list

**Train with:** JPEG, Gaussian Blur, Color Jitter, Center Crop — applied to **every** training image each epoch (`distortion_prob = 1.0`), not just a subset, with severity randomized per image.
**Hold out entirely from training:** Resize, Gaussian Noise.

**Why, with numbers:** "Improved Detection of Latent Diffusion-Generated Images" (arXiv 2410.11835) found training with common corruptions as augmentation produced gains of +37 to +52 points on post-processed test data versus not augmenting at all. More decisively, the NTIRE 2026 Challenge on Robust AI-Generated Image Detection in the Wild (CVPR 2026 workshop, 511 participants, 20 teams) — a near-identical task at much larger scale — found the top-scoring team's best run used `distortion_prob = 1.0`, applying a distortion to every training image as a deliberate strong regularizer, not an occasional one. This revises the original "~50% of images" guidance upward: go aggressive on the held-in transforms.

But if you evaluate only on the exact transforms you trained with, you can't distinguish "the model generalizes" from "the model memorized your recipe." Holding out two full transform types (Resize, Gaussian Noise) gives you an honest, reportable number for genuine generalization — and it's a specific, defensible methodological choice you can point to in your write-up under "Innovation & Problem Insight."

### 3.3 Feature-caching trick (this is what makes the timeline in Section 5 realistic)

Because the backbone is frozen, you don't need to run CLIP forward every epoch. Instead:

1. For each training image, generate one version **per (transform, severity) combination** in the held-in set — not a handful of random draws. The held-in set is JPEG (4 severities: quality 90/70/50/30) + Gaussian Blur (3 severities: σ 0.5/1.0/2.0) + Color Jitter (1 setting: ±20%) + Center Crop (1 setting: 80%) = **9 combinations**, plus 1 clean version = **10 versions per source image**. Random sampling risks under-exposing the model to the harshest severities purely by chance; deliberately covering every severity guarantees the model has actually trained on the exact extreme cases (JPEG q=30, blur σ=2.0) it will be evaluated on. This is more images to cache, but caching is a one-time cost (step 2 below), so the extra coverage is nearly free — roughly 10× your base training set size in cached embeddings, not 10× your training time.
2. Run all of them through frozen CLIP **once**, cache the resulting embeddings (as `.npy` or a single tensor file).
3. Train the linear/MLP head on these cached embeddings. Since it's just a small head on a few-hundred-to-1024-dim vector (Section 1.2), each epoch takes seconds, not minutes.
4. This turns "training with augmentation" from an expensive repeated-computation problem into a one-time preprocessing cost — critical for hackathon time budgets.

### 3.4 Beyond the brief's grid: stacked/combined distortions

The brief's own note says evaluation "will consider robustness against a subset of the following augmentations" — testing single transforms in isolation, per the grid in 3.1. That's the minimum bar, and Sections 3.1–3.3 already cover it fully, extremes included. But real images rarely go through exactly one distortion — a photo gets resized on upload, then re-compressed when reshared, then possibly cropped for a thumbnail. The NTIRE 2026 challenge (Section 1.1b) used 36 transformations, suggesting combined/sequential degradation is part of what "realistic" means in this space.

**Recommended addition, once the core grid (3.1–3.3) works end-to-end:** add a small number of stacked worst-case tests to your robustness evaluation — e.g. resize 0.25× → blur σ=2.0 → JPEG q=30 applied in sequence to the same held-out test images — and report these as an extra row or two beyond the required grid, clearly labeled as "combined, beyond-spec." This costs little (it's the same transform functions from 3.2, just chained), strengthens the Robustness Evaluation Summary deliverable with a harder, more realistic case, and is the kind of extra rigor that stands out under "Technical Execution" and "Innovation & Problem Insight" without being required.

## 6. Evaluation protocol summary

- **Primary metrics:** accuracy and AUC on the clean held-out test set. AUC is threshold-independent; accuracy requires a decision threshold on the sigmoid output — use 0.5 by default and state this explicitly, or calibrate on the validation set if you find the model's confidence scores are skewed, but document whichever you choose (this is separate from the `pred` value in the required JSON output, which is always the raw confidence score, not the thresholded label — Section 0).
- **Robustness metric:** accuracy per transform × severity cell, reported as a table (required deliverable), plus the delta from clean accuracy — the smaller the drop, the more robust.
- **Combined/stacked cases (Section 3.4):** report a small number of chained worst-case transforms separately from the required grid, clearly labeled as beyond-spec — don't mix these into the main table since they're not what the brief's grid asks for, but they strengthen the summary.
- **Held-out generalization claim:** report Resize and Gaussian Noise results _separately and explicitly labeled_ as "never seen during training" — this is your strongest evidence of genuine robustness rather than memorized augmentation, and it's free once the harness exists.
- **Backbone comparison (Phase 5):** report CLIP vs. DINOv2 (and DINOv3, if attempted) clean accuracy and robustness table side by side, not just your final chosen backbone's numbers — the comparison itself is evidence of thorough evaluation.

## 7. Stretch goals (only after Phases 1–6 are solid)

1. **LoRA fine-tuning instead of a frozen linear head — attempted and closed out (2026-08-27), not skipped.** `src/train_lora.py` was fully built (LoRA r=16 on the last 2 transformer blocks' `out_proj` + MLP linears via `peft`, 294,912 trainable params) with checkpoint/resume verified via an actual mid-epoch kill-and-resume test. Not run for a specific, evidence-based reason rather than left as an open stretch goal: the Phase 5 robustness sweep showed in-distribution performance already near-ceiling (nothing to gain from more capacity there), and the CIFAKE cross-generator gap turned out to be substantially a calibration/resolution-sensitivity issue (AUC survives distortion far better than fixed-threshold accuracy; ~28 of the ~48-point CIFAKE accuracy drop is attributable to resolution alone, isolated via a same-generator control) rather than a representation-capacity shortfall LoRA would address — and adapting further into SID_Set's single-generator (FLUX) distribution risked narrowing generalization further, not fixing the actual problem. See the Changelog for full reasoning and numbers. The published evidence motivating this (MoLE's +12.72%, NTIRE 2026's DINOv3+LoRA results) remains real and would be worth revisiting on a dataset/setup where the gap is shown to be capacity-driven rather than calibration-driven.
2. **CLIP vs. DINOv2/DINOv3 backbone comparison** (Section 1.1b) — **attempted and closed out (2026-08-27), not skipped**: on this project's CPU-only hardware, DINOv2 ViT-L/14 measured ~63x slower per image than CLIP (2.835 vs. 0.045 sec/image), driven by its 518x518/~1370-token input versus CLIP's 224x224/~50 tokens — a model-family property, not fixable by a smaller variant. Even a reduced-scope comparison (2,800 clean-only images) projected to ~2.2 hours, over budget for this hackathon. Dropped for lack of GPU access, not because the comparison stopped being worthwhile — remains a legitimate experiment for anyone with GPU access, and the exact cost numbers are logged in the Changelog for the README's limitations section.
3. **Frequency-domain feature fusion** — concatenating a DCT/FFT-derived feature to the CLIP embedding has published support for GAN-generated images specifically, but note: diffusion models (the more common case today) do _not_ reliably produce the same frequency artifacts as GANs — treat this as a GAN-specific enhancement, not a general one.
4. **Tampered/local-manipulation detection as a third class** — only if core deliverables are done early; this is a genuinely different, harder problem (Section 0).
5. **Cross-generator generalization check** — SID_Set's synthetic images are all FLUX-generated (Section 2.2), so a model trained purely on it risks overfitting to FLUX-specific artifacts rather than learning general synthesis cues. Evaluate your trained model (inference only — never train or fine-tune on this data, per Section 2.1/Section 8) on the brief's own WildFake COCO-vs-DALL·E subset — a different generator (DALL·E) and a different real-image source (COCO, not OpenImages) — to check whether performance holds up outside the training distribution on both axes at once. This doubles as using the demo-only subset exactly as the brief intends (a reference checkpoint), and directly strengthens "Impact & Relevance" by showing the result isn't a single-generator or single-real-source artifact. CIFAKE (Stable-Diffusion-based) remains a reasonable fallback cross-generator sample if WildFake's subset is inconvenient to obtain.

## 8. Known pitfalls checklist

- [ ] Checked the native resolution distribution between real and fake images in the raw dataset (Section 1.4) — not a training-augmentation issue since Resize is held out, but a raw-data bias risk that would surface as a false "robustness failure" during the Phase 5 Resize evaluation.
- [ ] Near-duplicate/leakage check done via pHash or embedding similarity before splitting (Section 2.2) — SID_Set's synthetic side is single-generator (FLUX), so this matters more than a generator-based split here.
- [ ] No image appears in more than one of train/val/test.
- [ ] "Tampered" images excluded from both training and evaluation, and this choice is stated explicitly in the written description.
- [ ] Never *trained* on the WildFake COCO-vs-DALL·E demo-only validation subset (no gradient updates, no fine-tuning). Evaluation/inference on it is fine and expected — it's the intended cross-generator generalization check (Section 7 item 5).
- [x] Model parameter count confirmed under 2B **and stated explicitly in the README** — final model is 151.3M total (151,277,313 frozen CLIP ViT-B/32 backbone + 513 trainable linear-probe params), confirmed by direct enumeration on the actual checkpoint, 2026-08-27. DINOv3 was never used, so its parameter-cap trap doesn't apply here.
- [ ] Random seed set and logged for the data split and for training (Section 2.2, Phase 4).
- [ ] Classification threshold for accuracy reporting stated explicitly (Section 6) — don't leave it implicit at 0.5 without saying so.
- [ ] No API keys or secrets committed to the repo.
